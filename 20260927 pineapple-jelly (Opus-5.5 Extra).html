Drawn on the CPU

Pineapple Jelly is built for WebGPU and WGSL shaders, but this browser could not verify a hardware WebGPU adapter. So the page switched to its CPU renderer.

The CPU renderer runs the same soft-body physics, grabbing and controls. The image comes from a software rasteriser running in parallel worker threads. It keeps the refraction, colour that depends on thickness, fibres, juice cells, bubbles, gloss and coloured shadow, but at a lower resolution and with fewer interior samples.

For the full WebGPU version, use a current Chrome or Edge (113+) or Safari 26 on a machine with a supported GPU. On Linux and some older GPUs, WebGPU may still be behind chrome://flags/#enable-unsafe-webgpu.

Implementation

      

        
Soft body
A cylindrical lattice of 40 × 4 × 3 = 480 nodes, split into 1,440 tetrahedra with 2,320 elastic edges. Small-step XPBD runs on the CPU at a fixed ≈840 substeps per simulated second, with one constraint pass per substep. Edge constraints stiffen with strain, like gelatin, so a hard pull spreads out instead of drawing out a spike. Signed tet-volume constraints use a tiny non-zero compliance, because a perfectly hard one pumps energy in. The simulation runs at the same rate whatever the frame rate; ¼ speed simply feeds it a quarter of the time.

        
Grabbing
The cursor ray is tested against a coarse copy of the deformed surface, so a click through the empty hole hits nothing and orbits the camera instead. A hit becomes a soft XPBD "pinch": lattice nodes are weighted by a Gaussian around the grabbed material point and pulled toward a plane facing the camera. The scroll wheel pushes the grab deeper or pulls it closer. Pulling moves the nearby flesh first, and the far side follows a moment later.

        
Contacts & damping
The floor has static and kinetic friction, and invisible stage walls keep the ring in view. Node-sphere self-contact, run every 7th substep, stops the ring from passing through itself and keeps the hole from being squeezed shut. Internal damping removes only the part of each node's velocity that differs from the best-fit rigid motion (Müller et al. 2007). Wobbles therefore die out, while falls, slides and flips carry on unchanged.

        
Visual surface
A separate, much finer ring of 360 × 104 = 37,440 vertices and 74,880 triangles, with 18 soft scallops on the outer edge and a rounded-rectangle cross-section. The same detail is on both cut faces. Each vertex is embedded in the lattice through a periodic cubic B-spline around the ring and cubic/quadratic Bézier bases across it. A WGSL compute shader re-evaluates this every frame and outputs the position, the normal (F⁻ᵀn) and the local deformation gradient's inverse F⁻¹.

        
Flesh
The fragment shader refracts the view ray (IOR 1.42) and marches it through the body in material space using F⁻¹. Radial fibres, elongated juice vesicles and air bubbles are defined in rest coordinates, so they stretch, bend and flip with the flesh. Absorption follows Beer–Lambert along the deformed path, so thinner, stretched areas let more light through. In-scattering uses a thickness map along the key light. A ray that leaves through the hole and meets the far side of the ring is followed through it, and total internal reflection gets one extra bounce.

        
WebGPU or CPU
On load the page detects what the browser can do. A hardware WebGPU adapter gets the full WGSL renderer. The page switches to its CPU renderer when WebGPU is missing, the adapter is software or its hardware identity is hidden, the device fails to start, no frame finishes within 4 seconds, or the GPU device is lost later. The CPU renderer is a software rasteriser running in parallel Web Workers, each taking an interleaved set of rows so the work stays balanced. It rasterises the nearest front and back faces of a 6,768-vertex copy of the ring and shades each pixel: thickness, Beer–Lambert colour, fibres, juice cells and bubbles along the entry→exit chord in material space, the refracted floor, Fresnel softbox reflections, and the coloured shadow. The floor away from the ring is cached while the camera is still, and the resolution adapts to frame time. The panel shows which renderer is running; ?renderer=cpu forces the CPU path.

        
Light & frame
The light is analytic: a warm studio sweep, soft rectangular softboxes that appear only in reflections, Schlick Fresnel, a clamped GGX highlight, and Khronos PBR Neutral tone mapping with no bloom. The floor shadow is tinted by the light that passes through the jelly and gets sharper where the ring touches the floor. A top-down occupancy map adds contact occlusion. Rendering uses 4× MSAA, a depth pre-pass, at most two frames in flight, and adaptive resolution when the frame rate drops. The tint presets change only the absorption and scattering colours; refraction, gloss and scattering strength stay the same.

      

    


    

      
Testing

      
The same simulation source runs headless in Node. Scripted checks drop, poke, nudge and flip the ring, pull the inner edge outward and across the hole, lift the outer edge, fling it, and set the sliders to their extremes. They look for NaNs, inverted tetrahedra, volume drift, the size of the hole and how long the ring takes to settle. A further 36 pull-and-release gestures (three grab points, six directions, slow and fast) all returned to the rest shape; the largest error after settling was 0.014 units.

      
The page itself was driven in headless Microsoft Edge with real mouse and touch events. The inner edge was stretched and released, the outer edge lifted, and the flip, reset, tints and mesh overlay were used. A click through the empty hole seen from above turns the view instead of grabbing. On a touch-emulated phone viewport, a one-finger drag grabs the jelly and a two-finger pinch zooms. The test machine has no hardware WebGPU, so the WebGPU renderer was checked on the SwiftShader software adapter by reading frames back from an offscreen texture. On that machine the page chooses the CPU renderer by itself.
