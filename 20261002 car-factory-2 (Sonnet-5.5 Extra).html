    What you are watching

    An electric sedan is built in nine stations across a 180 m hall. Two lanes run in parallel: the body is framed and welded, then painted, while the battery “skateboard” chassis is assembled alongside. An overhead hoist then marries them, and the car moves on through wheels, interior, glazing, exterior fit-out and a final test before it drives out of the hall.

    How it is built
    • Everything is a function of one clock. Parts, robots, cranes, cars, paint, lamps and sparks are all computed from the master time T, so the scrub bar works in both directions and any frame is reproducible.
    • The car is procedural. The body is one lofted surface defined by station cross-sections, then sliced into real panels (fenders, four doors, hood, roof, quarters, glass…). Wheel arches are cut in the parameter domain, so panel gaps and curvature line up. The skateboard (pack, motors, subframes, wishbone and multilink suspension, discs) and the interior are built from primitives.
    • Robots use analytic IK. Six-axis arms on rails solve the wrist position each frame and carry the part they hold; cranes follow their hook point. Weld sparks and arc glow are stateless GPU particles; the paint shader sweeps steel → primer → colour → clear coat.
    • Rendering. Physically based materials with a procedural factory environment map, planar floor reflections, a shadow-casting key light that follows the action, HDR bloom and filmic tone mapping. Quality Auto steps the effects down if the frame rate drops.
    • Direction. The auto-director cuts between the parallel lanes; drag to take over (Follow / Free), or ride in the Cockpit once the body is on the chassis. Click any part to inspect it.
    Honest simplifications
    • A real line fits roughly 5,000 spot welds and takes hours per car; the timeline is a ~3½-minute illustration, not a time-and-motion study.
    • Robots do not collision-check against the body; the dimensions and station order are plausible but invented.
